Design Rationale

I designed Can AI Translate This? as a decision-making tool rather than an explanation of how generative AI works. The five-step sequence gives Year 7–8 students a repeatable process: identify context, check accuracy, consider cultural meaning, protect private information, and make a final decision. The short sections and worked examples are intended for use during a lesson, not as background reading.

Pedagogical Design

The resource is anchored to AC9LC8EC05, which asks students to develop strategies to interpret, translate and convey meaning in familiar Chinese contexts. The curriculum elaborations themselves use 加油 to show why literal translation can fail. This makes AI translation a useful object of inquiry: students are still learning Chinese, but they also evaluate a digital output. Recent reviews of generative AI in language education and school AI literacy identify evaluation, critical use and ethics as recurring priorities, supporting a design that asks students to judge and revise rather than simply accept generated text (Li et al., 2025; Park, 2025).

Digital Safety and Human Agency

The Confidentiality step translates policy into an action students can perform. The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools identifies privacy, security and safety, transparency and accountability as guiding principles. This is particularly relevant because eSafety found that 32% of Australian children who had used an AI assistant or companion reported sharing personal or potentially sensitive information. The final CREATE step keeps responsibility with the learner: AI offers a possible translation; the student must keep, change or reject it and justify the decision. This also reflects UNESCO’s human-centred approach to generative AI in education.

Meeting the Unit Learning Outcomes

ULO1

Safe, responsible and ethical ICT use

Demonstrated through privacy, verification and responsible AI use.

ULO2

ICT-supported learning opportunities

Demonstrated by embedding AI evaluation within a curriculum-aligned Chinese learning activity rather than treating ICT as an add-on.

ULO3

Digital literacy for inclusive practice

Demonstrated through a repeatable process for evaluating digital information, recognising cultural context and retaining control over the final response.

Walk-Through Transcript

Hi, this is my Digital Education Portfolio. The site is organised into Home, Teaching Resources, Digital Citizenship, Inclusive Practice and Professional Learning. I kept the navigation and visual design consistent so each section can be reached directly from any page.

For this assessment, my main classroom resource is on the Digital Citizenship page. It is called Can AI Translate This? and is designed for students studying Chinese in Years 7 and 8.

The resource starts from a problem students could realistically encounter. AI translation tools can produce an answer almost instantly, and the answer can sound convincing. In language learning, though, translating the words does not always communicate the intended meaning.

I use 加油 as the first example. A literal translation could produce “add oil”, while in context a speaker may mean “come on”, “keep going” or “you can do it”. This example connects directly to AC9LC8EC05, where students develop strategies to interpret, translate and convey meaning in familiar Chinese contexts.

I organised the resource around five checks: Context, Check, Culture, Confidentiality and Create. I wanted students to have a short sequence they could actually use rather than a long list of rules about artificial intelligence.

Context asks students to consider the speaker, audience and situation. Check asks them to verify an AI output using another source. Culture asks whether tone, relationships or cultural meanings have been lost. Confidentiality reminds students to stop before sharing personal or private information with an AI system.

The final stage, Create, is the most important design choice. Students have to keep, change or reject the AI translation and explain why. The aim is to keep the student responsible for the final communication rather than treating the AI response as an answer.

This also connects to digital safety. The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools includes privacy, security and safety, transparency and accountability as guiding principles. Instead of presenting those ideas only as policy language, I translated them into actions a Year 8 student can perform.

The resource addresses ULO1 through safe, ethical and responsible use of AI. It addresses ULO2 because the digital tool is used within a specific Chinese curriculum learning activity. It addresses ULO3 because students practise digital literacy by checking information, considering privacy and making an independent judgement about an AI-generated response.

Overall, I wanted the resource to show that using AI critically can itself become part of language learning.
