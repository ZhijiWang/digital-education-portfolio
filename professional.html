Design Rationale

I first considered building the activity around obviously bad AI translations. I changed this because common expressions are often translated reasonably well by current tools. A more useful classroom problem is what happens when several translations look possible. The resource therefore asks students to compare options and decide which one fits a particular relationship and situation. The examples using 不好意思 and 辛苦了 keep the focus on language use rather than on catching AI making an easy mistake.

Pedagogical Design

The resource is anchored to AC9LC8EC05, which asks students to develop strategies to interpret, translate and convey meaning in familiar Chinese contexts. The task asks students to consider who is speaking, the purpose of the utterance, and what may be difficult to carry across languages. This keeps the language-learning goal central while also asking students to evaluate an AI-generated answer. Research on generative AI in language education and school AI literacy similarly emphasises evaluation, critical use and ethical judgement (Li et al., 2025; Park, 2025).

Digital Safety and Human Agency

The privacy section turns safety guidance into a simple decision students can make before using an AI tool: should this information be uploaded at all? The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools identifies privacy, security and safety, transparency and accountability as guiding principles. eSafety also reports that some Australian children have shared personal or potentially sensitive information with AI systems. The final translation choice stays with the student. AI supplies possible wording; the learner still decides what fits the context and explains why.

Meeting the Unit Learning Outcomes

ULO1

Safe, responsible and ethical ICT use

Students are prompted to check AI output and keep private information out of AI tools.

ULO2

ICT-supported learning opportunities

AI translation is used as material for a Chinese learning task in which students compare possible meanings in context.

ULO3

Digital literacy for inclusive practice

The resource uses short prompts, worked examples and a repeatable scaffold. The site also uses semantic headings, keyboard focus states, skip navigation and responsive layouts to support different ways of accessing the material.

Walk-Through Transcript

My main resource is Can AI Translate This?, designed for Year 7–8 Chinese students. I wanted the activity to start from a realistic problem. Current AI tools can often give a perfectly reasonable translation, so I did not want students simply looking for an obvious machine error. The harder question is whether a translation fits the situation.

I use 不好意思 because it shows this clearly. Depending on the context, it can be translated as “sorry”, “excuse me”, or “sorry to bother you”. The Chinese expression stays the same while the English changes. This lets students focus on who is speaking, their relationship, and what the speaker is trying to do.

I organised the page around three questions: what is happening, what other translation options are available, and what may be difficult to carry across languages. I chose questions instead of a long list of rules because I wanted students to have a process they could use again with another expression.

The task connects directly to AC9LC8EC05, which asks students to develop strategies to interpret, translate and convey meaning in familiar Chinese contexts. AI is useful here because it can generate plausible alternatives for students to compare, while the curriculum goal remains interpretation and communication.

The darker section of the page gives students another example, 老师，辛苦了。 Instead of giving one deliberately poor translation, I show three possible English versions. Students decide which one they would use after a lesson and whether they would rewrite it. That decision is where I want the human judgement to remain.

I also separated privacy from translation quality. Even if an AI tool gives a good answer, students still need to decide what information should be entered into it. The resource therefore tells students not to upload private messages, login details, photographs or another student’s work, linking the classroom activity to the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools and eSafety guidance.

This supports ULO1 through safe and responsible AI use, ULO2 by using AI within a curriculum-aligned Chinese learning activity, and ULO3 through the scaffolded questions and accessible website design. My aim is for students to use AI as one source of language options while keeping the final interpretation with the learner.
