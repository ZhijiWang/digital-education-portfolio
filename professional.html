Design Rationale

I first considered building the activity around obviously incorrect AI translations. I moved away from that approach because it creates an easy distinction between a “wrong” machine answer and a “correct” human answer that does not reflect how students are likely to encounter current AI tools. More often, the difficulty is that several translations are plausible. The resource therefore treats AI as a source of alternatives rather than as an unreliable opponent. Students must decide which wording fits the speaker, relationship and situation. This keeps human judgement central without assuming that AI output is automatically poor. A limitation is that the examples are curated: they teach a way of questioning AI output, but they do not reproduce the variability of a live model.

Pedagogical Design

The language-learning goal remains primary. AC9LC8EC05 asks students to interpret, translate and convey meaning in familiar Chinese contexts, so the task focuses on what an expression is doing in a particular interaction. Kramsch (1993) supports treating context and culture as part of meaning, while reviews of GenAI in language learning and AI literacy emphasise critical evaluation of generated output (Li et al., 2025; Park, 2025). Lee et al. (2025) further show that GenAI may default to dominant linguistic norms in multilingual English-language contexts. Their study does not provide direct evidence about Chinese–English translation, but it raises a relevant pedagogical problem: fluent output can narrow sociolinguistic variation. I therefore use AI to open comparison rather than to supply a model answer.

Digital Safety and Human Agency

The privacy section makes one risk visible before students use an AI tool. The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools places privacy, security and safety alongside transparency, fairness and accountability, and the 2024 Review endorsed by Education Ministers in June 2025 retained the Framework as current guidance. eSafety’s 2026 survey found that 32% of Australian 10–17-year-olds who had used AI assistants or companions reported sharing personal or potentially sensitive information. That finding is broader than classroom translation, so I use it to establish the relevance of privacy education, not to claim that this activity itself creates that risk.

Student caution is also only one layer of protection. Teachers and schools remain responsible for selecting appropriate tools and setting clear boundaries. The resource asks students to make informed decisions without suggesting that safe AI use is solely an individual responsibility.

Meeting the Unit Learning Outcomes

ULO1

Safe, responsible and ethical ICT use

Privacy, verification and teacher-approved tool use make safe and ethical ICT use explicit.

ULO2

ICT-supported learning opportunities

AI-generated alternatives become material for a curriculum-aligned Chinese task rather than replacing the language-learning task.

ULO3

Digital literacy for inclusive practice

Short prompts, worked examples, semantic structure and keyboard-accessible navigation support access, although these design choices do not remove the need for teacher differentiation.

Walk-Through Transcript

My main resource is Can AI Translate This?, designed for Year 7–8 Chinese students. I originally considered using an obviously poor AI translation and asking students to correct it. I changed that idea because it made the problem too easy. Current AI tools can often produce translations that are perfectly plausible. The more useful question is what students do when several answers could work.

That is why I use 不好意思. Depending on the situation, it might be translated as “sorry”, “excuse me”, or “sorry to bother you”. None of these is automatically wrong. Students have to look at who is speaking, their relationship, and what the speaker is trying to do. This connects directly to AC9LC8EC05 because the curriculum asks students to interpret and convey meaning in familiar contexts, not simply match one word with another.

I also wanted to avoid presenting AI as either a perfect answer source or a machine that students just need to catch making mistakes. Lee and colleagues discuss a related problem in multilingual English contexts, where GenAI can favour dominant linguistic norms. Their study is not about Chinese–English translation, so I am not using it as direct evidence for this specific language pair. It does, however, support the broader reason for asking students to compare generated language critically.

The activity therefore uses AI to create alternatives for discussion. In the 老师，辛苦了。 example, students see several possible English versions and decide which one fits the situation, or whether they would write something different. I want the language-learning decision to stay with the student.

Safety is treated separately from translation quality. A good translation does not make a tool safe to use with personal information. The privacy prompt asks students to stop before uploading private messages, login details, photographs or another student’s work. The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools places this within a broader set of responsibilities that includes schools, teachers and service providers, so I did not want the resource to imply that safety is only the student’s responsibility.

This design addresses ULO1 through explicit privacy and verification choices, ULO2 by using AI-generated language as material for a curriculum-aligned Chinese task, and ULO3 through short prompts, worked examples and accessible site structure. These features support access, but they do not replace the need for teacher differentiation. Overall, the resource uses AI as something students can work with and question while keeping interpretation, responsibility and classroom judgement visible.
