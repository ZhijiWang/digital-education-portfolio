Design Rationale and Professional Learning

These notes explain how I developed Can AI Translate This?, why I chose the examples, and how the activity connects to curriculum, safety and the unit learning outcomes.

Design Rationale

I started with a simple idea: give students a poor AI translation and ask them to fix it. When I tried to build the activity around expressions such as 不好意思 and 辛苦了, the task felt quite flat. AI can usually produce an English version that makes sense. The more difficult part comes when two translations both seem reasonable and the difference only becomes clear once the situation is known.

That became the basis of the final activity. Students see several possible translations, choose the one they would use in that situation, and explain their choice. I kept the examples fixed so everyone is looking at the same language during the discussion.

Pedagogical Design

This also comes from my own experience learning languages. I often know what a phrase means before I know when I would actually say it. 辛苦了 is a good example because the English wording changes depending on what has happened in the conversation.

Kramsch (1993) treats context and culture as part of meaning in language learning. That connects closely with AC9LC8EC05, where students interpret and convey meaning in familiar contexts. Park (2025) also identifies evaluation of GenAI output as part of GenAI literacy. In this activity, students practise that evaluation through the language task itself. They choose a translation and explain what makes it fit the exchange.

Digital Safety and Human Agency

I placed the privacy check before the AI activity because the first decision students make is what they enter into the tool. The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools and eSafety guidance both treat privacy as part of responsible AI use.

The resource now makes the different roles visible. Students decide what to enter and make the final language choice. The teacher selects an approved tool and explains how it can be used in class. School platform and privacy expectations set the wider conditions around that use.

Meeting the Unit Learning Outcomes

ULO1

Safe, responsible and ethical ICT use

The privacy check and approved-tool boundary place safe ICT use inside the activity itself.

ULO2

ICT-supported learning opportunities

AI provides several translations for students to examine within a Year 7–8 Chinese task aligned with AC9LC8EC05.

ULO3

Digital literacy for inclusive practice

The page uses short sections, worked examples and keyboard-accessible controls. In class, I can adjust the language support and examples for different learners.

Accessibility Transcript

Hi, so this is my Digital Citizenship resource, Can AI Translate This? It’s for Year 7 and 8 Chinese students.

The idea actually came from my own experience learning languages. Sometimes I know exactly what a phrase means, but when I have to say the same thing in another language, I still stop and think, “Would I actually say it like that?”

So at first, I thought I’d make a really simple activity. I’d give students a bad AI translation, and they would correct it.

But when I started looking for examples, I realised that was actually quite hard to do, because AI translation is often pretty good now. If I type something like 不好意思, it normally gives me something that makes sense. The more interesting problem is that it might give me two or three answers, and all of them look possible. So that became the activity instead.

Here, for example, I use 不好意思 in three different situations. In the first one, “sorry” works. Here, “excuse me” makes more sense. And here, it becomes “sorry to bother you”. So the students are seeing the same Chinese expression, but the English keeps changing. And that’s really what I want them to notice. The translation depends on what is happening in the conversation.

That also links quite naturally to AC9LC8EC05, because students are meant to interpret and convey meaning in familiar contexts.

Kramsch was useful for me here as well. Her work made me think more about context and culture as part of meaning itself. That really matches what I’ve experienced learning languages, because knowing the dictionary meaning is sometimes the easy part. Knowing when to actually use something is harder.

Then I wanted students to actually make a decision themselves. So down here, I use 老师，辛苦了。 The AI gives them a few possible translations, and they can click the one they would use.

I didn’t want the website to immediately tell them “correct” or “wrong”, because the interesting part is their reason. After they choose, it asks them what in the situation made that translation work for them.

That part also connects with Park’s work on GenAI literacy, especially the idea of evaluating AI output. Here, they’re doing that evaluation through a Chinese language task.

I also put the privacy reminder before the interactive activity. That was quite deliberate, because before students even think about whether the translation is good, they have to think about what they are putting into the AI tool. So the page reminds them to keep personal information out of it.

I also added this section about responsibility. The student decides what they enter and which translation they choose. As the teacher, I would choose the AI tool we use in class and explain the rules around it. And then the school has its own privacy and platform requirements as well.

For the ULOs, I think they come through quite directly in the resource. ULO1 is mainly the privacy check and the use of an approved AI tool. ULO2 is the translation activity itself, because the technology is actually being used as part of the Chinese learning task.

And for ULO3, I’ve kept the page quite simple to follow, with short sections, examples, and keyboard-accessible controls. In a real class, I could also change the language support depending on the students.

So overall, I didn’t want AI to be the thing giving students the answer. I wanted it to give them something to think about, and then the student still has to make the language decision.
