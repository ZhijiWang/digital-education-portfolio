Design Rationale and Professional Learning

These notes explain how I developed Can AI Translate This?, why I chose the examples, and how the activity connects to curriculum, safety and the unit learning outcomes.

Design Rationale

I started with a simple idea: give students a poor AI translation and ask them to fix it. When I tried to build the activity around expressions such as 不好意思 and 辛苦了, the task felt quite flat. AI can usually produce an English version that makes sense. The more difficult part comes when two translations both seem reasonable and the difference only becomes clear once the situation is known.

That became the basis of the final activity. Students see several possible translations, choose the one they would use in that situation, and explain their choice. I kept the examples fixed so everyone is looking at the same language during the discussion.

Pedagogical Design

This also comes from my own experience learning languages. I often know what a phrase means before I know when I would actually say it. 辛苦了 is a good example because the English wording changes depending on what has happened in the conversation.

Kramsch (1993) treats context and culture as part of meaning in language learning. That connects closely with AC9LC8EC05, where students interpret and convey meaning in familiar contexts. Park (2025) also identifies evaluation of GenAI output as part of GenAI literacy. In this activity, students practise that evaluation through the language task itself. They choose a translation and explain what makes it fit the exchange.

Digital Safety and Human Agency

I placed the privacy check before the AI activity because the first decision students make is what they enter into the tool. The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools and eSafety guidance both treat privacy as part of responsible AI use.

The resource now makes the different roles visible. Students decide what to enter and make the final language choice. The teacher selects an approved tool and explains how it can be used in class. School platform and privacy expectations set the wider conditions around that use.

Meeting the Unit Learning Outcomes

ULO1

Safe, responsible and ethical ICT use

The privacy check and approved-tool boundary place safe ICT use inside the activity itself.

ULO2

ICT-supported learning opportunities

AI provides several translations for students to examine within a Year 7–8 Chinese task aligned with AC9LC8EC05.

ULO3

Digital literacy for inclusive practice

The page uses short sections, worked examples and keyboard-accessible controls. In class, I can adjust the language support and examples for different learners.

Walk-Through Transcript

The idea for this resource came from something I have noticed while learning languages myself. Sometimes I understand a phrase quite well, but I still hesitate over what I would actually say in another language. 辛苦了 is a good example. Its English translation changes with the situation, so I wanted students to work with that uncertainty.

My first idea was to give students a bad AI translation and ask them to correct it. Once I started testing examples, that seemed too easy. AI can usually produce something understandable. The more interesting point is when several translations all seem possible. That is why the resource gives students a few options and asks them to choose the one they would use.

The page first uses 不好意思 in several situations so students can see how the English changes around the same Chinese expression. After that, they work with 老师，辛苦了。 and make their own choice. This connects directly with AC9LC8EC05 because the curriculum focuses on interpreting and conveying meaning in familiar contexts.

My own experience influenced this design, but I also used the literature to shape the task. Kramsch’s work helped me think about context and culture as part of meaning. Park’s review of GenAI literacy was useful for the next step because it identifies evaluation of AI output as an important skill. In this activity, students practise that through translation. They look at the options and explain why one suits the exchange.

I placed the privacy check before the AI activity because safe use begins with what students decide to enter. I also added a short section showing how responsibility is shared. Students control what they enter and make the language choice. As the teacher, I would choose a school-approved tool and explain the classroom rules. The school’s platform and privacy expectations provide the wider boundary for that lesson.

The same features connect to the unit learning outcomes. The privacy check addresses ULO1. The translation activity uses AI to create a curriculum-linked learning opportunity for ULO2. The worked examples and accessible controls support ULO3, with further adjustments made in class when particular students need more support.

Across the resource, AI gives students material to work with. The learning comes from the choices they make and the reasons they give for those choices.
