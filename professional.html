Design Rationale and Professional Learning

These notes explain how I developed Can AI Translate This?, why I chose the examples, and how the activity connects to curriculum, safety and the unit learning outcomes.

Design Rationale

I began with a correction task: show students a bad AI translation and ask them to fix it. Once I tried to build examples around 不好意思 and 辛苦了, that felt too easy. AI usually gives something understandable for expressions like these. The harder part is choosing between several translations that all look reasonable until the situation becomes clear. I rebuilt the task around that problem. Students compare the options and explain which one they would actually use. I kept the examples fixed so the class can stay with the same language and discuss the choices behind it.

Pedagogical Design

Learning another language has made me realise that knowing the meaning of a phrase often comes before knowing how to use it. 辛苦了 is a good example. I understand what it means in Chinese, but choosing an English translation depends on the conversation around it. That is why I wanted the activity to begin with a situation rather than with a sentence on its own.

Kramsch (1993) describes language learning in a similar way, placing the cultural context of communication within language learning itself. This helped me connect the task to AC9LC8EC05, where students are already expected to interpret and convey meaning in familiar contexts. The AI translations then give students something to test against that context.

Park’s (2025) review was useful for deciding what students should actually do with the AI output. Critical evaluation is one of the GenAI literacy competencies identified in the review, and it is also an area where students have difficulty. For this resource, evaluation happens through a language decision: students choose a translation and explain why it works in that particular exchange.

Digital Safety and Human Agency

I separated privacy from translation quality because a fluent answer can make an AI tool feel more trustworthy than it is. Before students use AI, the resource asks them to check what they are about to upload. This follows the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools and eSafety guidance on young people sharing personal information with AI systems. For this activity, students keep private messages, login details, photographs and other students’ work out of the tool. The teacher still decides which tools are appropriate and sets the conditions for their use. Students make the language choices, while responsibility for a safe learning environment remains shared with the teacher and school.

Meeting the Unit Learning Outcomes

ULO1

Safe, responsible and ethical ICT use

The privacy check, teacher-approved tool rule and verification step give students a concrete routine for safe and responsible ICT use.

ULO2

ICT-supported learning opportunities

AI-generated translations become material for a Year 7–8 Chinese task aligned with AC9LC8EC05. Students interpret and discuss the output instead of simply accepting it.

ULO3

Digital literacy for inclusive practice

Short sections, worked examples and keyboard-accessible navigation make the task easier to follow. In class, I would still adjust examples and support for individual learners.

Walk-Through Transcript

My resource is Can AI Translate This?, for Year 7–8 Chinese. I started with a simple correction activity where students would find a bad AI translation and fix it. Once I began choosing examples, that approach felt less useful. With expressions such as 不好意思, AI will usually give several answers that are all understandable. The real question is which one belongs in the conversation.

That is why the page begins with the same Chinese expression in different situations. Students can see that 不好意思 may become “sorry”, “excuse me” or “sorry to bother you” depending on how it is being used. From there, they move to the 老师，辛苦了。 example and choose the English they would actually use. I wanted the task to feel like a language lesson first. AC9LC8EC05 is about interpreting and conveying meaning in familiar contexts, and this gives students a reason to look beyond a dictionary meaning.

My own experience learning languages influenced this choice. I often find that understanding the words comes earlier than knowing what sounds natural in a particular situation. Kramsch’s work on language and culture helped me think about that more clearly. I also used recent GenAI research to think about what students should do with generated language. Lee and colleagues discuss dominant linguistic norms in multilingual English contexts. I would not treat that as direct evidence about Chinese–English translation, but it made me more careful about presenting fluent AI output as neutral.

The privacy check comes before students are asked to use an AI tool. I placed it there because translation quality and safe use are different questions. A useful answer can still come from a tool that students should not give personal information to. The resource gives them a clear boundary around private messages, login details, photographs and other students’ work. The teacher also chooses the tool and sets the conditions for using it, so safety is not left to students alone.

For ULO1, those privacy and verification steps make responsible ICT use visible in the task. ULO2 is addressed through the use of AI-generated translations as material for a curriculum-aligned Chinese activity. For ULO3, I used short sections, worked examples and keyboard-accessible navigation, and I would still adapt the examples and support in class for particular learners.

The main decision running through the resource is that AI supplies language to examine, while students still have to make sense of it and explain their choice.
