0:00–1:30 · The audit. Hi, this is my digital education portfolio for Part B. I want to start with the activity I made for Part A, Can AI Translate This?. It is for Year 7 and 8 Chinese. Originally, I had three examples showing that 不好意思 can mean different things in English. Students then selected a translation for another classroom situation.

When I revisited that page, I found a few practical problems. I had given students Chinese text with no way to show pinyin. The question was a long way down the page. And after a student clicked an answer, I asked them why they chose it, without giving them a place to type their reason.

Here on the Inclusive Practice page, I've put those points in a table. You can open each Before and After link. The revised activity keeps the original examples, adds a pinyin switch, and lets students choose between two familiar situations. At the end, they can write their reason and copy or print it. Those changes came from thinking about the three UDL areas: access to information, choice, and how students show their learning.

1:30–3:30 · The new task. Now I'll open the Translation Evidence Lab. This is a separate task about checking digital information. Here I can choose a classmate asking for help with a photo, or a student thanking a teacher. I'll use the photo example.

The student sees the Chinese, a suggested English translation, and the situation. The suggestion sounds fine at first. It says “Thanks for sending it” while the classmate has not sent anything yet. That small detail matters. I want students to spot it, rather than just deciding which English sentence sounds nicer.

The example translations were written by me, which the page states clearly. Students then explain what they would need to check before trusting one. They can look up a word in an approved dictionary and record what they found. It is quite a guided activity. For this age group, I think that makes sense as a first attempt at checking a source. Students then write their own version and explain it. When I read their answers, I would look at whether they used the situation and whether they understood what the dictionary could actually tell them.

3:30–4:50 · What this demonstrates. I can show the three unit outcomes through different parts of the site. For ULO1, the examples are invented, the sources are labelled, and students don't need an AI account. For ULO2, the digital page keeps the translation, context and checking notes together. I use a similar test-and-explain approach in photography, where students compare camera settings, and in coding, where they run and check what code actually does. For ULO3, the audit shows the changes I made after looking at what might stop students from using the first activity.

I'd like to try the two pages with students and see what they find confusing. In particular, I want to know whether they can explain why they trust one piece of evidence more than another. That would be useful feedback for my next version.

Academic notes · Design decisions

Decisions I would defend

Giving everyone the same simulated translation keeps the lesson manageable and avoids an unapproved AI service. Ducar and Schocket (2018) argue that machine translation deserves explicit attention in language teaching. My first Evidence Lab prototype still supplied its own conclusion in the teacher note. I rewrote that note as a question. Students now have to identify what Source A leaves out and record the check they would make.

I considered giving students a list of search engines. For beginning Chinese learners, choosing and evaluating unfamiliar websites would create another substantial task. A teacher-approved dictionary gives them a starting point; they can record its finding or explain why access was unavailable. McGrew et al. (2018) examined civic online reasoning, so the relevance to translation is methodological: a source-checking process needs to be visible. Later, students could choose and compare sources themselves.

The remaining question is whether the supports work in class. Source inspection confirms labelled inputs, Chinese language tags and keyboard focus styling. The primary text on the page background has a calculated contrast ratio of about 13.6:1. These checks establish properties of the implementation. Student observation and screen-reader testing would provide different evidence about usability.
