Inclusive audit and retrofit

Hi, this is my Part B digital education portfolio. I'll start with Can AI Translate This?, the activity I designed for Year 7 and 8 Chinese in Part A. In the original activity, students looked at different translations of the same Chinese expression and chose one that suited a classroom situation.

When I went back through the website, I noticed a few problems. The main question was quite far down the page. There was no pinyin support, and students could select a translation without having anywhere to write their explanation.

On my Inclusive Practice page, I've included before-and-after screenshots of these changes. I added shorter steps, optional pinyin, two classroom situations to choose from, and a response box. These changes were informed by the CAST UDL guidelines.

In a Chinese classroom, students have different levels of experience with this language. Some may already read Chinese characters comfortably, while others still need pronunciation support. I also wanted students to have different ways to explain their thinking, either in writing or orally.

One thing I would still need to check in class is whether students can use these features independently. I would observe when they struggle and adjust the activity based on that.

The new learning task

Now I'll move to my new activity, the Translation Evidence Lab. In Part A, students were mainly choosing between translations I had already provided. For this new task, I want them to investigate the evidence before making that choice.

I'll show you the photo example. Here, Source A suggests, “Could you send the photo to the teacher? Thank you for sending it.” That sounds quite natural in English, but Source B tells us that the classmate hasn't replied yet. So the translation seems to assume something has already happened. Students need to notice that difference and decide whether the suggestion is appropriate.

They can also look up 麻烦 in a dictionary and record what they find. The dictionary might explain the meaning of the expression, but it cannot tell students whether the photo has actually been sent. They still need to consider the context.

This is where the work by McGrew and colleagues on evaluating online information connects to my design. I want students to show what they checked and how they reached their decision.

I deliberately wrote the AI-style examples myself and labelled them as simulations. That lets me control the classroom example, while students still have responsibility for evaluating them.

I'll open the response section here. Students record a credibility question, their source check, their own translation and the reason for their choice. The website prepares their response, but it doesn't automatically mark it. I would use their explanation to understand how they are thinking and give feedback.

This is also an extension for students who are more confident in Chinese. They can compare expressions such as 麻烦了, 麻烦你了, 谢谢 and 辛苦了, and explain how these meanings change in different situations.

Unit learning outcomes and next steps

Finally, I'll explain how the portfolio meets the three unit learning outcomes. For ULO1, I use fictional examples, clear source labels and privacy reminders. Students can complete the task without creating an AI account.

For ULO2, the digital activity supports a specific learning goal: evaluating a translation using language evidence and context. I can apply a similar approach in my other teaching areas. In Photography, students can compare an edited image with the original. In Games Coding, they can test suggested code and explain whether it works as expected.

For ULO3, the Inclusive Audit shows how I revised the activity to support different learners through optional pinyin, clear steps and different ways to explain an answer.

If I use this activity in class, I would pay particular attention to the evidence students chose. If they rely only on dictionary definitions, I would bring another example of the same expression into the next lesson and ask them to reconsider their translations. This is how I would use their responses to explain what to teach next.

Academic notes · Interpreting student work

What I would look for

Two students could write the same translation after making very different checks. The final English sentence wouldn't tell me that. McGrew et al. (2018) found that evaluating online sources was difficult for many students, so I ask them to record what they checked. I would read that record alongside their English before deciding what to revisit in class.

Unit learning outcomes

Where the evidence sits

ULO 1 · APST 4.5

Safe and ethical ICT

The ethical and privacy design connects to APST 4.5 (Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership [AITSL], 2011). Students work with labelled examples and the site has no submission server. See the privacy reminder.

ULO 2 · APST 2.6

ICT across subjects

APST 2.6 (AITSL, 2011) guides ICT integration. Students check a translation against the Chinese. In Year 8 Photography, they can compare their Photopea edits with the original photograph. In Games Coding, they can run and check suggested Godot movement code. See the Chinese task.

ULO 3

Inclusive digital literacy

The audit records the pinyin switch, shorter steps and a response field. Students can also explain their answer orally through a teacher-supported route. See the audit.
