0:00–1:30 · The retrofit. Hi, this is my Part B digital education portfolio. I'll start with Can AI Translate This?, the activity I made for Year 7 and 8 Chinese. The original used three examples of 不好意思 to show how the same phrase can change in English. Students then chose a translation for another classroom situation.

Going through the page again, I found three points I wanted to fix. There was no pinyin support, the question sat a long way down the page, and students could click an answer without recording their reasoning. On Inclusive Practice, the Before and After links show where I changed each part. I'll open the revised resource here and turn pinyin on so you can see the support beside the Chinese text. The revised activity has shorter steps, an optional pinyin switch and a place to write a response. Students can choose between two school situations and keep their explanation for feedback. I used CAST's UDL guidelines to plan those changes.

1:30–3:30 · The new learning task. Now I'll open the Translation Evidence Lab. This is a new information-literacy activity. I'll select the photo example. Source A suggests, “Thanks for sending it.” Source B tells us the classmate hasn't replied yet. Source C asks students what they need to investigate. They can look up 麻烦 in an approved dictionary and record what they find. That dictionary may give “bother” or “trouble.” Source B tells us that the classmate has not replied yet. It gives us no evidence that the photo has already been sent, so students have to question the thank-you in Source A.

I wrote the example translation myself and labelled it clearly. Students decide which evidence they trust, write their own English and explain why it suits the conversation. That is where student agency sits in the task. My role is to choose suitable material, set the safety rules and read their explanations. I'll show the response box here: it brings their credibility question, dictionary finding and revised English into one record. I use that record for feedback. The website prepares their response; it doesn't assign a score.

For students who already use Chinese confidently, I've added an extension comparing 麻烦你了, 谢谢 and 辛苦了. They work with the same question of audience and context, at a deeper level.

3:30–4:45 · Unit outcomes. ULO1 is visible in the source labels, invented examples and the privacy check. For ULO2, students investigate a translation using a digital source-checking process. I use similar thinking in Photography when students test camera settings, and in Games Coding when they run and check code. ULO3 is represented by the changes shown in my audit, especially optional pinyin and the written response.

When I use this with students, I'll look closely at the evidence they cite. If they rely on a word definition alone, I'll bring another context for the same expression into the next lesson. Their responses will tell me where to teach next.

Academic notes · Interpreting student work

What I would look for

In the photo example, Source A thanks the classmate for sending the picture, although Source B shows that they have not replied. The English sounds natural, which makes the mistake easy to miss. Ducar and Schocket (2018) examine how the strengths and limitations of machine translation can be addressed in language teaching. I use this example to get students to check what the suggested translation assumes about the conversation. McGrew et al. (2018) found that students often struggled to evaluate online claims and evidence. I have brought that question into the translation task: what evidence is there that the photo has already been sent?

The dictionary check raises a different issue. Students might find that 麻烦 means “trouble” and translate 麻烦你了 as “sorry to trouble you.” That tells me something about their vocabulary knowledge, but I would also want to know how they understood the request. Liddicoat and Scarino (2013) approach language learning through the interpretation of meaning in social and cultural contexts. In this case, I would look at whether students considered the relationship between the classmates and the timing of the message.

Where the evidence sits

ULO 1 · APST 4.5

Safe and ethical ICT

The ethical and privacy design connects to APST 4.5 (Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership [AITSL], 2011). Students work with labelled examples and the site has no submission server. See the privacy reminder.

ULO 2 · APST 2.6

ICT across subjects

APST 2.6 (AITSL, 2011) guides ICT integration. The Chinese task checks a translation claim. The same approach can test edited images in photography or suggested code in Games Coding. See the Chinese task.

Inclusive digital literacy
