0:00–1:30 · The audit. Hi, this is my digital education portfolio for Part B. I want to start with the activity I made for Part A, Can AI Translate This?. It is for Year 7 and 8 Chinese. Originally, I had three examples showing that 不好意思 can mean different things in English. Students then selected a translation for another classroom situation.

When I revisited that page, I found a few practical problems. I had given students Chinese text with no way to show pinyin. The question was a long way down the page. And after a student clicked an answer, I asked them why they chose it, without giving them a place to type their reason.

Here on the Inclusive Practice page, I've put those points in a table. You can open each Before and After link. The revised activity keeps the original examples, adds a pinyin switch, and lets students choose between two familiar situations. At the end, they can write their reason and copy or print it. Those changes came from thinking about the three UDL areas: access to information, choice, and how students show their learning.

1:30–3:30 · The new task. Now I'll open the Translation Evidence Lab. This is a separate task about checking digital information. Here I can choose a classmate asking for help with a photo, or a student thanking a teacher. I'll use the photo example.

The student sees the Chinese, a suggested English translation, and the situation. The suggestion sounds fine at first. But it says “Thanks for helping” even though the classmate hasn't agreed to help yet. That small detail matters. I want students to spot it, rather than just deciding which English sentence sounds nicer.

All of the AI-style suggestions here were written by me. The page says that clearly. Then students can check a word with a dictionary approved by their teacher, and record what they found. It is quite a guided activity. For this age group, I think that makes sense as a first attempt at checking a source. Students then write their own version and explain it. When I read their answers, I would look at whether they used the situation and whether they understood what the dictionary could actually tell them.

3:30–4:50 · What this demonstrates. I can show the three unit outcomes through different parts of the site. For ULO1, the examples are invented, the sources are labelled, and students don't need an AI account. For ULO2, the digital page makes it possible to keep the translation, context and source-checking notes together while students make a Chinese language decision. For ULO3, the audit shows the changes I made after looking at what might stop students from using the first activity.

I'd like to try the two pages with students and see what they find confusing. In particular, I want to know whether they can explain why they trust one piece of evidence more than another. That would be useful feedback for my next version.

Academic notes · Design decisions

Where the research changed my plan

McGrew et al. (2018) studied students' ability to evaluate online information in civic contexts. My task is much smaller: a short Chinese classroom exchange. Still, their work prompted a useful question. What can a student actually check? The first version of my evidence lab had three cards, all written by me. Students could compare them, yet they had no independent source. I added a dictionary lookup and a space for recording the finding or explaining why the check was unavailable. The next challenge would be letting students choose a source themselves.

The dictionary introduces another problem. It can give meanings for 辛苦, but it cannot establish what a student means when thanking a teacher after class. Kramsch’s (1993) account of language in context helped me keep that distinction in the task. This shaped my decision about feedback as well: a multiple-choice score would say little about a student's understanding of the relationship between speakers. I need to read the reason they give. For the next lesson, I would group common explanations and ask students to reconsider a phrase in a different situation.

CAST (2024) informed the retrofit of my Part A page. I added pinyin, split the reading into steps, and made it possible to keep a written response. Those are changes I can point to. Whether they help the students I teach is still an open question. I would check the page with keyboard users and ask students where they get stuck. If students keep missing the reflection, for example, changing its placement may be more useful than adding another support feature.
