The Translation Evidence Lab

Teaching design · Assessment evidence

Learning intention & success criteria

Learning intention

Students examine a suggested translation, identify what needs checking, and use source evidence to explain an English version that fits the conversation.

Success criteria

  1. Explain what they would check before trusting Source A.
  2. Compare the suggestion with the situation and a named dictionary or approved source.
  3. Write their English version and use a detail from the conversation to justify it.

Access and differentiation

Students can turn on pinyin, choose a school situation and use the writing prompt. They can also explain their translation to me in a short conference or record it through a school-approved platform. I would ask for the same source check and explanation either way. Students with more Chinese can compare 麻烦你了, 谢谢 and 辛苦了.

Formative check

I would look for a named source, a finding from the check, and a reason tied to the audience. If students copy a dictionary definition without using the context, we would revisit the phrase in two different conversations.

Pedagogical justification · Approximately 500 words

Why I designed it this way

In my first resource, Can AI Translate This?, students chose a translation and explained it. I had supplied most of what they needed to decide. In this new task, I wanted them to identify what a convincing English sentence leaves uncertain. They record a credibility question, consult another source, and explain a revision.

Ducar and Schocket (2018) argue for teaching students to work critically with machine translation in language classes. I considered using a live translator, which could give students varied outputs. Access would depend on school approval, and the class might receive very different examples. For the first lesson, I wrote two plausible suggestions and labelled them as simulations. Students then check a phrase using a dictionary or another approved source.

McGrew et al. (2018) examined how students evaluate online information. Their work prompted me to make the checking process visible in this Chinese task. Students write down what they need to know about Source A, consult a named source and decide which evidence supports their interpretation.

In the photo case, Source A thanks the classmate for sending an image, while the context says the classmate has not replied. Students have to decide whether that difference matters. The dictionary offers information about the expression; it cannot settle the timing of the exchange.

Liddicoat and Scarino (2013) place interpretation and intercultural meaning-making at the centre of language learning. This informed my choice of 麻烦你了 and 辛苦了. A dictionary may clarify a word while the English sentence still depends on the speaker's purpose. Students compare the wording with the situation, addressing AC9LC8EC05 (Australian Curriculum, Assessment and Reporting Authority [ACARA], 2022). I would accept different versions supported by an explanation.

TPACK (Mishra & Koehler, 2006) helped me match the technology to the learning task. Students keep the Chinese, source labels and checking notes together as they revise their English. I read their exported explanations and give feedback on how they used the conversation.

AC9LC8EU03 also addresses comparing features of Chinese and English (ACARA, 2022). In a mixed Chinese classroom, some students may already speak Chinese at home, while others are learning to read the characters. Optional pinyin and writing prompts offer different entry points (CAST, 2024). Students with more experience can compare three expressions in an extension. Everyone still has to justify meaning in context.

After the lesson, I would group responses by the evidence students used. If students relied on a dictionary definition alone, I would compare two uses of the same expression in the next class. I could put two anonymous explanations on the screen, ask the class which one accounts for the context, and have students revise their own answer. That second response would show whether the discussion changed their reasoning. Students would submit through a school-approved platform, following the privacy guidance in the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (Australian Government Department of Education, 2023).

Classroom implementation

Suggested lesson flow

05 MIN

Model the source check

Take one short phrase and show why a dictionary definition may be insufficient.

20 MIN

Work through the lab

Pairs check the source and dictionary. Each student then writes their own explanation.

10 MIN

Discuss and revise

Discuss two student versions, then check the reasoning in their submitted notes.
