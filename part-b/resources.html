The Translation Evidence Lab

An evidence-checking mini-investigation for Year 7–8 Chinese. Students challenge a plausible AI translation, trace the context and defend a revision.

Teaching design · Assessment evidence

Learning intention & success criteria

Learning intention

Students evaluate the credibility and contextual fit of a digital translation claim, then communicate a reasoned English revision.

Success criteria

  1. Identify the origin of the AI-style example.
  2. Record one independent dictionary finding or a verification limit.
  3. Revise the translation and explain its suitability for the speaker and audience.

Access and differentiation

Optional pinyin, two familiar contexts, clearly labelled evidence cards, and writing prompts support students at different stages of Chinese learning. Students may explain their decision orally using the same criteria.

Formative check

I would review the saved responses for accurate source identification, a credible independent check, and a context-based explanation. If students rely only on a dictionary definition, the next lesson begins with a paired comparison of two contexts.

Pedagogical justification · Approximately 500 words

Why I designed it this way

I developed this activity after noticing a problem with my first resource, Can AI Translate This?. Students could choose between plausible translations, but their reasoning was difficult to examine. The revised task asks them to trace a suggestion to its source, check a Chinese expression independently, and explain why a particular English version fits a familiar exchange.

I considered letting students generate translations using a live chatbot. That would provide varied answers, although access would depend on school approval and the responses would be harder to compare. I chose two teacher-authored, explicitly labelled simulations for the first lesson. Students work from the same examples and can focus on checking a claim. I then added a separate dictionary lookup: comparing only my prepared cards would have left all the evidence under my control.

McGrew et al. (2018) found that students often struggled to evaluate online claims and evidence. Their work prompted me to make source provenance an assessable part of the task. Students identify the simulated output, consult an external dictionary or approved classroom source, and record what the check establishes. A word definition may help with vocabulary; the original exchange still supplies the speaker's intention. I would gradually remove the named-source prompt once students can select and justify their own checking sources.

This also addresses the Chinese learning goal. Kramsch (1993) connects language use to cultural and conversational context, while AC9LC8EC05 expects learners to interpret and translate meaning in familiar situations. I chose 麻烦你了 and 辛苦了 because their English equivalents change with audience and purpose. A literal translation is sometimes defensible; students need to explain why it suits this speaker at this moment.

In TPACK terms (Mishra & Koehler, 2006), the language problem determines the teaching strategy and the technology choice. Evidence cards keep different claims visible. Students can revisit a source, change their wording, and export a record of their decision. I avoided automated right-or-wrong feedback because the teacher needs to judge whether the explanation fits the context.

CAST's (2024) UDL guidelines shaped the retrofit. Pinyin can be revealed when helpful; the task is divided into steps; and responses can be copied, printed or explained orally. These options keep the same success criteria while offering different routes through the activity. I would observe whether learners use the supports effectively before claiming an accessibility improvement.

For formative assessment, I would sort responses by the quality of their source check and contextual reasoning. If students treat a dictionary definition as sufficient proof, the following lesson would compare two uses of the same phrase. Student work would be submitted only through a school-approved channel. The page itself uses invented examples and no AI login, supporting the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools' principles of privacy, transparency and teacher responsibility (Australian Government Department of Education, 2025).

Classroom implementation

Suggested lesson flow

05 MIN

Model the source check

Demonstrate how context changes one possible translation.

20 MIN

Work through the lab

Pairs identify source origin, check an expression in a dictionary, and record a reasoned revision.

10 MIN

Discuss and revise

Compare two justified answers and check whether the sources support each explanation.
