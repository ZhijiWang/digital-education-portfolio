The Translation Evidence Lab

Would you trust a translation because the English sounds natural? In this Year 7–8 Chinese lesson, students check the source and the situation before choosing their words.

Two resources, two purposes. Can AI Translate This? is my revised Part A activity. The Translation Evidence Lab is the new Part B task. The teaching notes below are about the new task.

Teaching design · Assessment evidence

Learning intention & success criteria

Learning intention

Students check who produced a translation, test one word against a dictionary, and decide whether the English fits the conversation.

Success criteria

  1. Recognise that the AI-style example was made by the teacher.
  2. Record what a dictionary says, or explain why they could not access it.
  3. Write an English version and explain how the situation influenced it.

Access and differentiation

Students can turn on pinyin, choose between two school situations, and use a writing prompt. If writing is a barrier, they can explain their translation to me using the same evidence questions.

Formative check

I would look for a named source, a finding from the check, and a reason tied to the audience. If students copy a dictionary definition without using the context, we would revisit the phrase in two different conversations.

Pedagogical justification · Approximately 500 words

Why I designed it this way

In my first resource, Can AI Translate This?, students selected a translation and were asked why it worked. I realised that I had given them very little to check. They could answer by instinct, and I would still have trouble seeing how they reached the decision. In this new task, they have to record what they checked before writing a revised translation.

I considered using a live AI translator. It would be interesting to compare different outputs, although I would have less control over what a Year 7 class received. Students would also need access to an approved tool. For this lesson, I wrote two plausible translations myself and labelled them as simulations. Everyone starts with the same material. The next step is an independent dictionary check, because relying entirely on my example cards would make the investigation too easy to take at face value.

McGrew et al. (2018) studied how students evaluate online sources. Their research made me look more closely at what counts as checking in this task. Reading another answer is not enough. Students now identify where the suggested translation came from, look up a word, and record what the dictionary helped them establish. A dictionary gives information about a word; it cannot tell them what a particular speaker intended. I want students to notice that limit.

For the Chinese component, I drew on Kramsch’s (1993) discussion of language and cultural context. I chose 麻烦你了 and 辛苦了 because their English wording depends on what has happened between the speakers. The activity addresses AC9LC8EC05 through that translation decision. I would accept different versions when students can explain the choice using the situation.

TPACK (Mishra & Koehler, 2006) helped me check whether the digital format was doing useful work. The page keeps the original message, source labels and dictionary prompt close together. It lets students revise their answer and keep the explanation for feedback. An automatic correctness score would be misleading here, so I left the judgement with the teacher.

I used CAST’s (2024) UDL guidelines when deciding where students might need support. Pinyin is optional, the instructions are short, and students can copy, print or explain their answer aloud. These changes need testing with students. I would check which parts they still ask me to clarify.

After the lesson, I would read their source notes and explanations. If many students rely on a dictionary definition alone, I would bring two different uses of the phrase to the next class. The website uses invented examples and collects no answers itself. Students would submit through a school-approved platform, consistent with the privacy guidance in the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (Australian Government Department of Education, 2025).

Classroom implementation

Suggested lesson flow

05 MIN

Model the source check

Take one short phrase and show why a dictionary definition may be insufficient.

20 MIN

Work through the lab

Pairs check the source and dictionary. Each student then writes their own explanation.

10 MIN

Discuss and revise

Discuss two student versions, then check the reasoning in their submitted notes.
