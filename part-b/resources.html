The Translation Evidence Lab

An evidence-checking mini-investigation for Year 7–8 Chinese. Students challenge a plausible AI translation, trace the context and defend a revision.

Pedagogical justification · Approximately 500 words

Why I designed it this way

I developed this task from a problem I noticed while making Can AI Translate This?: an AI translation can be grammatically convincing even when it does not quite fit the exchange. In the first resource, students selected a version and explained their preference. This time, I ask them to check what supports that judgement. Their finished response names the evidence they used.

The task sits within the unit theme Information Literacy & Critical Investigation. Students work with a short Chinese message, a possible AI translation, and two other pieces of information: who is speaking and a teacher-curated note on the expression. They must decide which source is most useful for this particular translation decision. The source labels matter. The AI-style output is identified as a simulation, and the language note is identified as teacher-created. Students are encouraged to check the wording against a class dictionary or ask their teacher when a claim remains uncertain. I want them to notice how the authority of a source depends on what they are trying to establish.

For Chinese, the learning focus is interpreting and conveying meaning in familiar contexts (AC9LC8EC05). I selected 麻烦你了 and 辛苦了 because a literal translation can sound slightly awkward, while several natural alternatives may be defensible. Students use audience, time and purpose as evidence. They write their own English sentence and a short explanation. This gives me more useful formative evidence than a multiple-choice answer alone: I can see which detail they used and what they still misunderstand.

TPACK (Mishra & Koehler, 2006) helps explain the fit between the tool and the lesson. Language knowledge determines the problem; guided source comparison is the teaching strategy; the interactive page keeps the evidence visible as students work. The website does not automatically grade a nuanced language choice. I would review a sample of responses with the class, inviting students to defend or change their translations.

I applied CAST’s (2024) UDL guidelines while rebuilding the activity. Students can choose a school situation, turn pinyin on, read one piece of evidence at a time, and produce a response they can copy or print. A student who prefers to explain a choice orally can discuss it with the teacher using the same evidence prompts. These options support participation while preserving the common learning goal. I would check whether students can use them independently before treating the retrofit as successful.

The safety decision is deliberate. The page contains invented examples, stores no responses on a server and needs no student account. For classroom use, I would collect work only through a school-approved submission method. This follows the privacy, accountability and transparency expectations of the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (Australian Government Department of Education, 2025).

Classroom implementation

Suggested lesson flow

05 MIN

Model the source check

Demonstrate how context changes one possible translation.

20 MIN

Work through the lab

Pairs compare evidence; each student records a reasoned translation.

10 MIN

Discuss and revise

Compare two justified answers and give teacher feedback.
