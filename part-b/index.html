Professional statement

My approach to digital teaching

I speak several languages, and I still stop over simple expressions when I translate them. Take 辛苦了. “You have worked hard” is a possible English rendering. After a teacher has stayed back to help, I would probably say “Thank you for your help.” I want Chinese learners to notice that difference. Kramsch (1993) helped me think about language as something people use with one another, in a particular setting, rather than as sentences to match word for word.

Digital tools are useful here because students can look closely at competing answers. In my new translation task, they see a suggested version, read the situation, and check a word in a dictionary before writing their own English. I can then ask why they chose that wording. Mishra and Koehler’s (2006) TPACK framework has been useful in planning this: the Chinese learning goal comes first, the evidence-checking discussion gives the lesson its shape, and the website keeps the material easy to compare.

Teacher agency matters when I select a tool and decide what evidence to ask for. I wrote the translation examples myself so students could examine the same language in class. Student agency comes when they challenge a suggestion, consult another source and choose their own English. Their explanations help me decide what we should practise next.

That work has to be accessible. My first website assumed students could read the Chinese examples without pronunciation support. The revised version has a pinyin switch and breaks the activity into shorter parts. CAST’s (2024) UDL guidelines informed those changes. Students can use the support when they need it, and the question they answer stays the same.

I would also give students a chance to revise after hearing another student's explanation. A second choice can show what they understood from the discussion. It gives me a better starting point for feedback than a simple right-or-wrong score.

I also want students to understand the limits of the tools they use. The AI-style translations on this website are my own examples, labelled as such. Students are asked to check them, and they do not need an AI account. The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (Australian Government Department of Education, 2023) informed that decision. In class, I would collect their work through an approved school platform and use their explanations to decide what we should practise next.
