Professional statement

My approach to digital teaching

When I went back to my first Chinese translation activity, I noticed something I had missed while making it. Students could choose an answer and read the follow-up question, but they had nowhere to write their explanation. I had also put the question quite far down the page. It was easy for me to find because I knew where everything was. I hadn't thought enough about what the page would look like to someone opening it for the first time.

I wanted students to think about why a translation works in a particular situation. I speak several languages myself, and expressions such as 辛苦了 still make me stop and think. If a teacher stayed back to help me, I would probably say “Thanks for staying back” in English. “You have worked hard” gives the literal meaning, but it sounds different in that conversation. Kramsch (1993) discusses how language and cultural context shape meaning. This is something I want students to pay attention to when translating.

I changed the first activity so students could write down their reasoning. I also added optional pinyin and divided the page into shorter steps. In a Year 7–8 Chinese class, students will have different experiences with the language. Some may speak Chinese at home, while others are still learning to read the characters. Pinyin gives those students some support without changing the translation question they are working on. I used CAST's (2024) UDL guidelines when reviewing these choices.

For the new Translation Evidence Lab, I wanted to give students more to investigate. In the photo example, the English suggestion thanks a classmate for sending something, although the classmate has not replied yet. Students have to notice that detail, check the Chinese expression and decide what they would say. I wrote the suggested translations myself and labelled their origin. Students can complete the task without using an AI account, which also follows the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools (Australian Government Department of Education, 2023).

I chose a website because students can keep the Chinese message, suggested translation and checking notes in front of them while they work. This is how I have applied TPACK (Mishra & Koehler, 2006) to the task. I choose the examples and decide which tools are suitable for the class. Students can disagree with a suggested translation and explain their own choice. I would read those explanations before planning the next lesson, especially to see whether students are using the situation or relying on a dictionary definition alone.
